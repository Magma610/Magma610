Anyone arriving on Etna's south side set on reaching the volcano risks driving straight through Pedara without really stopping: a roundabout, a road sign, and off towards the craters. Yet the village deserves more than a passing glance. Pedara isn't a town built for tourism, with shopfronts designed for passers-through: it's a real village, with its own daily rhythm, its own hours, and its own square, where people still stop to chat in the evening after dinner.

## The main square and the centre

Pedara's main square is where village life is concentrated, especially in the evening: bars with outdoor tables, the ice cream shop that stays open late in summer, groups of older residents sitting on benches recounting their day, children playing between one streetlamp and the next while their parents chat nearby. Around the square sit the parish church, a few grocery shops, a pharmacy, and the odd historic bar that seems not to have changed in decades.

Strolling in the evening, it's not unusual to hear Sicilian dialect mixing with Italian at the bar tables, or to stumble upon the weekly market that fills a few streets in the centre each morning with stalls of fruit, vegetables and local produce. It's an atmosphere that tells the story of inland Sicily far better than the more famous coastal destinations ever could.

## Pedara as a base for exploring

Pedara's location isn't just convenient for village life: it's also an excellent starting point for exploring the surrounding area. Etna's trails and viewpoints are 20-30 minutes away by car, the wineries of the south side are just as close, and the Ionian coast — with Taormina and Catania — is barely half an hour away. In short, Pedara lets you alternate, within the same stay, the quiet of village life with excursions to the volcano, the wineries or the sea, without having to choose an isolated base at the expense of one or the other.
